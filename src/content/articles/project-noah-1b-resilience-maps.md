The House of Representatives says the 20th Congress secured ₱1 billion for Project NOAH in the 2026 national budget, placing hazard science and mapping inside the country’s disaster-resilience agenda. Speaker Faustino “Bojie” Dy made the statement during a November 25–26 summit in Isabela focused on local climate action, nature-based solutions, and sustainable development. The allocation is not a promise of a specific new consumer service. It is political backing for scientific tools that help local governments understand risks before floods, droughts, and other hazards become emergencies.

![DOST Project NOAH resilience workshop](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/project-noah-1b-resilience-maps/image-1.webp?v=202611270805)

The summit also featured the turnover of UP NOAH resilience maps to the provincial government and a presentation of updated high-resolution hazard and resilience maps. Those outputs matter because disaster planning depends on location-specific information rather than broad national warnings alone. A detailed map can show which communities, roads, farms, and public facilities face exposure. Used with local planning and early-warning systems, the information can guide evacuation routes, infrastructure priorities, and decisions about where limited resilience funds should go first.

![Philippine disaster-risk mapping workshop](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/project-noah-1b-resilience-maps/image-2.webp?v=202611270805)

Project NOAH grew from the University of the Philippines Resilience Institute’s work to make hazard and climate information more accessible to communities. The House statement connects that work to a wider technology question: whether public agencies can turn research into tools that local officials and residents can understand and act on. Maps do not remove risk, and they cannot replace evacuation procedures or emergency response. Their value comes from making those decisions more informed, repeatable, and easier to coordinate across agencies.

![DOST Project NOAH identity mark](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/project-noah-1b-resilience-maps/image-3.webp?v=202611270805)

The next challenge is implementation. Funding must support updated data, technical staff, reliable public access, and partnerships between UP, national agencies, provinces, and municipalities. The Isabela summit’s focus on local interventions suggests that the policy conversation is moving beyond national plans toward usable community systems. For Filipino households, farmers, and local governments, Project NOAH will be judged by whether its maps reach people before danger arrives. The technology becomes meaningful when a warning changes a decision early enough to protect lives and livelihoods.
