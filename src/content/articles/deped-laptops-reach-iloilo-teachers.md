The Department of Education has begun distributing a new wave of laptops to public-school educators, starting with an initial turnover of 300 MacBook Neo units in Iloilo City. President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. and Education Secretary Sonny Angara led the November 27 handover at Mabini Elementary School. The rollout is part of DepEd’s wider computerization program, which has procured 231,725 laptops for teachers and 3,646 units for administrative personnel. The Iloilo deployment covers teaching and non-teaching staff across 22 public schools, making the device program a practical classroom-infrastructure story rather than a simple product giveaway.

![Iloilo teachers seated with newly distributed laptops](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/deped-laptops-reach-iloilo-teachers/image-1.webp?v=202611281117)

DepEd says the devices are being paired with GuroXL hands-on training so educators can build confidence using digital tools in daily work. The department also describes the laptops as coming with productivity software, a five-year warranty, professional training, and Mobile Device Management software. Those details matter because a computer program is only useful when schools can maintain the hardware, support teachers, and manage devices after the handover. Training can help turn the units into tools for lesson preparation, communication, assessment, and collaboration instead of equipment that sits unused when technical questions arise.

![Public-school educators gathered during the Iloilo laptop rollout](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/deped-laptops-reach-iloilo-teachers/image-2.webp?v=202611281117)

The national scale of the procurement also shows the size of the operational challenge. Delivering more than 231,000 teacher laptops requires coordination across suppliers, regional offices, schools, inventories, connectivity conditions, and support teams. Teachers do not work in identical environments, so a device that performs well in one campus may face different power, network, or classroom constraints elsewhere. DepEd’s rollout will therefore be judged not only by the number of units distributed, but by whether teachers receive timely repairs, useful training, secure accounts, and reliable access to the systems the department expects them to use.

![Teacher receives a laptop during the DepEd turnover ceremony](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/deped-laptops-reach-iloilo-teachers/image-3.webp?v=202611281117)

For public-school educators, the Iloilo turnover offers a visible signal that digital capacity is becoming part of the job’s basic infrastructure. A laptop can reduce preparation friction, expand access to learning resources, and make administrative work easier, but it cannot solve gaps in broadband, power, staffing, or instructional support on its own. The next phase will be the less visible work of keeping devices updated, protecting student and teacher data, and measuring whether technology improves classroom practice. The program’s long-term value will depend on that support continuing after the ceremony ends.
