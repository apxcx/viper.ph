The Philippines is preparing for a severe El Niño while farmers already report drought losses in parts of the country. BusinessWorld reported that the Department of Science and Technology is urging local governments to adopt locally developed technologies for drinking water and irrigation. The goal is not to replace normal rainfall or solve every agricultural problem. It is to give communities practical tools before shortages become worse. The approach includes solar-powered irrigation, mobile water treatment and data-supported farm advisories, linking Philippine science programs to immediate needs in food production and water security.

![Researchers monitoring a weather station in an agricultural landscape](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/dost-technology-el-nino-philippines/image-1.webp?v=202611230851)

One of the highlighted tools is SAFEWTRS, a mobile and solar-powered treatment system developed by the DOST-Industrial Technology Development Institute. The system can treat rainwater, seawater, spring water and other sources through filtration, treatment and disinfection. BusinessWorld reported that each unit can produce as much as 2,000 liters of drinking water daily, enough for about 1,000 people, and that at least 35 units have been deployed across 11 regions. Solar-powered desalination systems are also being installed in coastal and island communities.

![Weather and irrigation monitoring equipment supporting climate research](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/dost-technology-el-nino-philippines/image-2.webp?v=202611230851)

For farms, DOST is promoting solar-powered irrigation systems that can be installed permanently or moved on trailer units. The technology can help farmers manage water when rainfall is unreliable, but its effect will depend on local water sources, maintenance and the cost of moving equipment. BusinessWorld said DOST considers locally developed systems cheaper to operate and maintain than imported alternatives. That flexibility matters in the Philippines, where island geography, uneven infrastructure and different crop conditions make a single national solution unrealistic.

![Solar-powered agricultural monitoring station in a sugarcane field](https://viper.ph/api/media/articles/2026/11/dost-technology-el-nino-philippines/image-3.webp?v=202611230851)

Data tools are another part of the response. Project SARAI combines satellite imagery, automated weather stations, geographic information systems and crop models to provide localized forecasts, drought advisories and crop-management information. BusinessWorld reported that the platform is being used by local governments and the Department of Agriculture in 80 provinces. Project NOAH and the PANaHON mobile app can also provide public weather and rainfall information. These systems improve planning, but wider deployment still requires partnerships among agencies, LGUs, communities and private groups.
