The Department of Science and Technology–Philippine Nuclear Research Institute is exploring whether Carabao mango can be developed as food for long-duration space missions. The project, called the Philippine Space Mango Program, brings together DOST-PNRI, the University of the Philippines Diliman, the Philippine Space Agency and the Batangas Mango High Value Growers Agriculture Cooperative. Researchers are examining how a familiar Philippine crop could become a shelf-stable product that travels well, carries cultural meaning and supports future missions beyond Earth. The work is still exploratory, but it gives a Philippine crop a place in a serious research conversation.

The work is expected to be featured during National Science, Technology, and Innovation Week from November 25 to 29 in General Santos City. DOST-PNRI project leader Gina B. Abrera said the goal is not only to solve a nutrition problem. The program also looks at how a Filipino food can give future astronauts a sense of identity and comfort. That approach connects food science with the country’s ambition to send its first astronaut into space by 2035. The partnership also makes the research easier to connect with growers, universities and national space planning.

Turning fresh mango into a space-ready product will require careful work on processing, shelf life, nutrition and packaging. Space food must remain safe and stable for long periods while fitting the operational limits of a mission. The project therefore gives Philippine researchers a practical test case for combining nuclear science, food science, agriculture and space policy. It also creates a public-facing example of how local crops can become part of advanced technology programs. It also gives students and farmers a visible reason to connect local science with international goals.

The possible benefits extend back to Earth. DOST says the research could strengthen the competitiveness of the Philippine dried-mango industry in international markets and inform food solutions for emergencies and disaster response. The program also gives growers and universities a role in a high-visibility national project. If the research succeeds, Carabao mango could represent Philippine agriculture in space while supporting new products, stronger supply chains and more resilient food planning at home. That broader value could matter even if the space application remains experimental for years.
