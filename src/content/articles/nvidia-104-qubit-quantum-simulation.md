NVIDIA says researchers have completed a record**104-qubit simulation**of fault-tolerant quantum computing on JUPITER, Europe’s first exascale supercomputer. The simulation models how a realistic quantum processor behaves when hardware noise and error correction are included. NVIDIA describes it as the largest comprehensive simulation of a fault-tolerant quantum system to date. The work is not a new quantum computer available for purchase. It is a high-performance computing experiment intended to help researchers test designs and understand the resources needed before building future quantum hardware.

The project focused on magic-state cultivation, a process that fault-tolerant quantum computers repeatedly use during computation. NVIDIA says the approach validated in the simulation can require 90% fewer physical qubits than previous methods. That claim concerns the resource demands of the method under study, not a promise that practical quantum computers are ready. By modeling realistic noise, researchers can examine how errors affect performance and compare design choices before committing to expensive hardware experiments. The result is a planning and validation tool for quantum research.

NVIDIA said the work used GH200 superchips at the Jülich Supercomputing Centre and scaled development from four GPUs to hundreds before moving to 8,192 GPUs on JUPITER. Researchers used the company’s cuQuantum software and CUDA-Q platform to express and accelerate the circuits. NVIDIA says a process that once required weeks was reduced to days during development and testing. These details show how GPU clusters and exascale systems can support quantum research even though they do not replace quantum processors or eliminate the engineering problems of building them.

The immediate significance is better evidence about how future quantum designs may behave under noise. NVIDIA says the simulations can inform work in drug discovery, financial modeling and materials science, but those applications remain longer-term goals. Researchers still need to build, control and error-correct physical quantum systems. The announcement therefore marks a computing milestone rather than a commercial breakthrough. It shows accelerated classical simulation being used to narrow the design space and shorten the feedback cycle before larger fault-tolerant quantum machines are constructed.
